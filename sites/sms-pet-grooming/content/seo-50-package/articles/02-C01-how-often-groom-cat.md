# How Often Should You Groom a Cat? A Schedule by Coat, Age and Lifestyle

There is no single grooming interval that suits every cat. **Long-haired, silky or curly coats often need daily brushing**, while many short-haired cats can be maintained with brushing once or twice a week. Professional appointments depend on coat condition, nails, matting, age, mobility and how well the cat tolerates handling.

SMS Pet Grooming presents **every four to six weeks** as a general routine booking guide. That can be a useful starting point, but it should not be applied mechanically. A healthy short-haired cat may need professional grooming less often, while a Persian, senior cat or cat developing mats may need help sooner.

## Home grooming and professional grooming are different schedules

Owners often ask how often a cat should be “groomed” without separating two different activities.

### Home grooming

Home grooming includes regular brushing or combing, checking for tangles, monitoring the paws and nails, and noticing changes in the skin or coat. This may take only a few minutes, but consistency is important.

Regular brushing removes loose hair, reduces debris and helps owners find mats before they become tight. It also reduces the amount of hair the cat swallows during self-grooming.

### Professional grooming

A professional appointment may combine bathing, drying, brushing, nail care, ear and eye-area cleaning, deshedding, dematting or trimming. The need for these services develops at a different rate from ordinary home brushing.

A cat may be brushed daily but still benefit from professional grooming every few weeks. Another cat may have an easy short coat and mainly need occasional nail and hygiene care.

## Grooming frequency by coat type

### Short-haired cats

Short coats usually do not tangle as quickly as long coats. Many short-haired cats can be maintained with brushing once or twice a week, although dense coats and heavy shedders may need more.

A short-haired cat may still need professional grooming when:

- loose hair becomes difficult to manage;
- the cat cannot reach part of the body;
- nails are becoming overgrown;
- the coat becomes greasy or dirty;
- the cat needs a professional bath; or
- the owner cannot safely complete the care at home.

### Medium-haired cats

Medium coats benefit from brushing several times a week. Friction areas behind the ears, under the front legs, around the groin and behind the back legs should be checked regularly.

Professional appointments can be scheduled before tangles become mats. Waiting until the coat is tightly matted usually reduces the number of comfortable grooming options.

### Long-haired, silky and curly-coated cats

These coats commonly require daily brushing or combing. The top of the coat can look smooth while tangles develop closer to the skin, so the coat must be checked in layers.

Persians and other dense long-haired breeds often need a structured professional routine. Four to six weeks can be a practical starting point, but the correct timing depends on the cat’s coat, bathing needs, tolerance and home maintenance.

### Hairless cats

Hairless cats do not need brushing for loose coat, but they still require skin, nail and ear care. Their schedule should be based on oil build-up, the products recommended for the individual cat and veterinary guidance when skin problems are present.

## How age and mobility change grooming needs

### Kittens

Kittens benefit from short, positive grooming introductions. The main goal is to make gentle brushing, paw handling and body checks familiar before a full appointment is necessary.

A kitten’s first professional visit should consider health, vaccination status, coat need and the groomer’s policy. It should not be treated as a test the kitten must “pass.”

### Adult cats

Healthy adults often maintain much of their coat themselves, but self-grooming does not replace regular owner checks. Indoor cats may also need more nail monitoring because natural wear can be limited.

### Senior cats

Older cats may groom less effectively because of arthritis, dental pain, obesity, weakness or other health conditions. Greasy fur, mats along the back or hindquarters, and overgrown nails can appear when mobility declines.

A sudden change in grooming habits should not be dismissed as normal ageing. It may be an early sign that the cat is uncomfortable or unwell.

Lifestyle changes several parts of the grooming schedule.

Indoor cats may have less natural nail wear and can shed at lower levels throughout the year because lighting and temperature are relatively stable. Outdoor cats may collect debris, burrs or parasites and should have the coat inspected after outdoor activity.

Outdoor cats also use their claws for climbing and escape. Nail decisions should therefore consider the cat’s lifestyle rather than applying the same schedule to every animal.

## Signs your cat needs grooming sooner

Move the appointment forward when you notice:

- tangles that are becoming difficult to separate;
- mats behind the ears, under the legs or around the hindquarters;
- unusually heavy loose coat;
- nails catching in fabric or curving excessively;
- greasy, dirty or unpleasant-smelling fur;
- reduced self-grooming;
- difficulty reaching the back or hindquarters;
- litter or waste becoming caught in the coat; or
- increased resistance that may indicate pain.

Redness, wounds, swelling, sudden bald areas, persistent itching or marked behaviour change should be assessed by a veterinarian.

## What a professional cat-grooming appointment can cover

The appropriate package depends on the cat’s current needs.

A routine appointment may include coat and hygiene care, bathing and drying, brushing and nail care. A more complete appointment may include broader trimming or shaving when the coat condition and requested style justify it.

## Build a realistic schedule for your cat

Use these five questions:

1. How long and dense is the coat?
2. How often is the cat brushed thoroughly at home?
3. Is the cat shedding, tangling or developing mats?
4. Can the cat still groom every area comfortably?
5. Which professional services are actually needed?

Start with a four-to-six-week review point for a cat that needs regular professional care. Then shorten or lengthen the interval based on what the coat and nails look like before the next appointment.

The best schedule is the one that prevents problems rather than repeatedly correcting them.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do indoor cats need professional grooming?

Some do and some do not. Indoor cats may need professional help with nails, loose coat, bathing, long-hair maintenance or areas they cannot groom properly.

### How often should a long-haired cat be brushed?

Long-haired, silky and curly coats often need daily brushing or combing to prevent tangles and mats.

### Is every four to six weeks too often?

Not for a cat that needs regular bathing, trimming, nail care or dense-coat maintenance. A short-haired cat with a manageable coat may need professional care less frequently.

### Does shedding change the grooming schedule?

Yes. Increase brushing during a heavy shed and consider professional deshedding when loose coat becomes difficult to remove safely at home.

### Should a cat be bathed at every grooming appointment?

Not automatically. Most healthy adult cats rarely need routine bathing. Bathing should match the coat, contamination, mobility and any veterinary treatment plan.
